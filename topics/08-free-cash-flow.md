# Free Cash Flow: FCFF and FCFE
{: .no_toc }

*~10 min read*

**🎯 Interview frequent**

## Why it matters

Accounting net income is distorted by non-cash charges, revenue recognition timing, and discretionary accruals. Equity analysts and lenders look past GAAP earnings to **Free Cash Flow (FCF)**—the cold, hard cash generated by core operations that could be extracted from the company without impairing its future productive capacity. In investment banking and private equity interviews, walking through Unlevered Free Cash Flow line by line is universally tested. Knowing whether to discount a given cash flow stream at [WACC](../04-wacc/) or at the [Cost of Equity](../03-capm-cost-of-equity/) separates junior analysts from seniors.

## Core concepts

- **Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF / Unlevered FCF):**
  The cash generated by core operations available to **all capital providers** (senior debt, mezzanine lenders, preferred equity, common equity) before any financing payments (interest or debt principal).
  - **Standard Formula (Starting from Operating Profit / EBIT):**
    \[
    \text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT} \times (1 - t) + \text{D\&A} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta\text{NWC}
    \]
    Where \(\text{EBIT}(1 - t)\) is **NOPAT** (Net Operating Profit After Tax), representing the firm’s hypothetical after-tax operating profit if it were entirely debt-free.
  - **Starting from Net Income:**
    \[
    \text{FCFF} = \text{Net Income} + \text{Non-Cash Charges} + \text{Interest Expense}(1 - t) - \text{CapEx} - \Delta\text{NWC}
    \]
    *(Interest expense is added back net of tax because interest is a financing cost paid to debt holders, not an operational expense.)*
  - **Starting from Cash Flow from Operations (CFO):**
    \[
    \text{FCFF} = \text{CFO} + \text{Interest Expense}(1 - t) - \text{CapEx}
    \]
- **Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE / Levered FCF):**
  The residual cash flow remaining **strictly for common shareholders** after satisfying operating expenses, taxes, working capital requirements, capital expenditures, and contractual net debt service (interest and principal repayments).
  - **Formula:**
    \[
    \text{FCFE} = \text{Net Income} + \text{D\&A} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta\text{NWC} + \text{Net Borrowing}
    \]
    Where \(\text{Net Borrowing} = \text{Debt Issued} - \text{Debt Repaid}\).
- **The Line-by-Line Mechanics:**
  - **+ Depreciation & Amortization (D&A):** A non-cash expense deducted on the income statement to match asset wear over time. While it reduces taxable income (providing a tax shield), no physical cash left the business in the current period, so it is added back.
  - **- Capital Expenditures (CapEx):** Real cash invested to purchase property, plant, equipment (PP&E), and capitalized software. Recorded on the Cash Flow Statement under investing activities; it bypasses the income statement directly to the balance sheet.
  - **- Change in Net Working Capital (\(\Delta\text{NWC}\)):** Working capital ties up operational liquidity.
    - An increase in operating current assets (e.g., Accounts Receivable, Inventory) is a **cash outflow** (cash is locked up in unpaid customer invoices or unsold stock).
    - An increase in operating current liabilities (e.g., Accounts Payable, Accrued Expenses) is a **cash inflow** (suppliers have effectively lent the company interest-free cash).
    - Therefore, \(\Delta\text{NWC} = \text{NWC}_t - \text{NWC}_{t-1}\) is subtracted.
- **The Golden Rule of Valuation Consistency:**
  - Discount **FCFF** at **WACC** \(\implies\) yields **Enterprise Value (EV)**.
  - Discount **FCFE** at **Cost of Equity (\(r_e\))** \(\implies\) yields **Equity Value**.
  - *Never cross the streams:* Discounting FCFF at \(r_e\) or FCFE at WACC is an immediate interview failure.

## Mental model

```
  Revenue
    - Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
    - Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D)
    ---------------------------------------------------------
  = Operating Income (EBIT)
    - Taxes on Operating Profit [EBIT * t]
    ---------------------------------------------------------
  = NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax)
    + Depreciation & Amortization (D&A)
    - Capital Expenditures (CapEx)
    - Investment in Net Working Capital (Delta NWC)
    =========================================================
  = Unlevered Free Cash Flow (FCFF)  ---> Discount at WACC -> Enterprise Value
    - After-Tax Interest Expense [Interest * (1 - t)]
    + Net Borrowing [New Debt Issued - Debt Repaid]
    =========================================================
  = Levered Free Cash Flow (FCFE)    ---> Discount at r_e  -> Equity Value
```

Unlevered cash flow represents the output of the operational engine regardless of who owns the vehicle; levered cash flow is the cash left in the owner's wallet after paying the monthly car loan.

## Interview questions

1. **Walk me through the calculation of Unlevered Free Cash Flow starting from Operating Income (EBIT).**  
   Answer: Start with EBIT. Multiply by \((1 - \text{Tax Rate})\) to arrive at NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax), which isolates after-tax operating earnings on a debt-free basis. Add back non-cash charges, primarily Depreciation and Amortization (D&A). Subtract Capital Expenditures (CapEx) to account for required ongoing capital reinvestment. Finally, subtract the Change in Net Working Capital (\(\Delta\text{NWC}\)). The resulting figure is Unlevered Free Cash Flow (FCFF).

2. **Why do we add back after-tax interest expense \([\text{Interest} \times (1 - t)]\) when calculating FCFF from Net Income, but not when calculating from EBIT?**  
   Answer: EBIT is calculated *before* interest expense has been subtracted on the income statement; therefore, EBIT is already capital-structure neutral. Net Income, however, is calculated *after* deducting interest expense, which reduced the earnings by \(\text{Interest} \times (1 - t)\) due to the tax shield. Because FCFF belongs to all capital providers (including debt holders), we must reverse that interest deduction by adding back the after-tax interest expense.

3. **If Accounts Receivable increases by $25M, Inventory decreases by $10M, and Accounts Payable increases by $15M, what is the net impact on Free Cash Flow?**  
   Answer:  
   Operating Current Assets: \(\Delta\text{AR} = +\$25\text{M}\) (cash drain), \(\Delta\text{Inventory} = -\$10\text{M}\) (cash source). Net \(\Delta\text{Assets} = +15\text{M}\).  
   Operating Current Liabilities: \(\Delta\text{AP} = +\$15\text{M}\) (cash source).  
   \(\Delta\text{NWC} = \Delta(\text{Operating Assets}) - \Delta(\text{Operating Liabilities}) = (25 - 10) - 15 = 15 - 15 = \$0\text{M}\).  
   The net change in NWC is zero, so the net impact on Free Cash Flow is **$0**.

4. **Can a company have positive GAAP Net Income but negative Free Cash Flow? Give two realistic business scenarios.**  
   Answer: Yes, frequently:  
   - **Hyper-growth / Working Capital Squeeze:** A rapidly scaling e-commerce or manufacturing company books large revenues and accounting profits, but customers pay on 90-day credit terms while the firm aggressively builds inventory to meet surging demand. The massive increase in Accounts Receivable and Inventory drains cash (\(\Delta\text{NWC} \gg \text{Net Income}\)).  
   - **Heavy Capital Expenditure Phase:** A telecom building out a 5G network or a semiconductor manufacturer constructing a new fabrication facility records high positive Net Income today, but cash CapEx far outstrips non-cash D&A.

5. **What is the difference between Maintenance CapEx and Growth CapEx, and why does it matter in valuation?**  
   Answer: Maintenance CapEx is the capital spending required to sustain existing productive assets at their current operational capacity and offset natural physical depreciation. Growth CapEx is the discretionary capital invested to expand operations, enter new markets, or increase capacity. In terminal value calculations, the business is assumed to reach steady-state maturity; thus, Growth CapEx declines toward zero and CapEx converges to Depreciation.
